# Findings: Thermal Envelope as a Passive Grid Asset

This is the results summary. For setup and project structure, see [`README.md`](./README.md). For the full research discipline behind these numbers, see [`PROJECT.md`](./PROJECT.md). For every citation, see [`citations.md`](./citations.md). Every figure below is reproducible by hand from `configs/tenure_insulation_assumptions.yml` in under a minute — that's the rule this project holds itself to.

**Revision note:** this version incorporates two rounds of external technical review, plus Week 5 and Week 6 extensions. The first round's most consequential change is in Finding 1 — the original single-node thermal model overstated how long original fabric can safely coast, and a more realistic two-node check shows the "3x margin" claim doesn't survive scrutiny for unretrofitted homes. Finding 6 extends the model to a BESS+solar+VPP fit-out, the natural comparator once Finding 5 established that fabric's own flexibility value isn't currently contractible. Finding 7 is new: it moves from the single −3°C design-condition event to a whole-year view — annual displaced energy, bill savings, carbon abatement, and a qualitative look at two summer risks the model has not tried to quantify. Finding 6 also gained a landlord-finance addendum: real 2026 compliance dates (MEES's extension to social housing, the reformed Decent Homes Standard) reframe fabric retrofit, for an HA landlord, as an asset-protection-and-compliance investment rather than an energy one — with one link in that chain (EPC status affecting balance-sheet valuation) explicitly flagged as reasoned, not sourced. Finding 8 is also new: it extends Findings 5 and 7 into a lifetime view, using a sourced 36-year fabric life (Ofgem ECO/Green Deal convention, replacing an earlier unsourced round-number guess) to show the retrofit is not merely cheap abatement but net cash-positive over its working life once lifetime bill savings are properly netted against the capital cost, in line with how a standard marginal abatement cost curve treats fuel savings. A second, shorter review round then checked two more things: whether the model accounted for a household's own internal heat gains (it didn't — now fixed, and it shifts every coastdown and peak-kW figure in Findings 1, 2 and 5 below, detailed there) and whether the baseline heat-loss coefficient was artificially low (it wasn't — checked and confirmed unchanged, see Finding 1). None of this flips the direction of any finding; the retrofit case gets stronger, not weaker, each time it's checked harder.

## The question

A pre-1919 solid-wall terrace — a cold, leaky, hard-to-treat slice of England's housing stock — sits on a mixed-tenure estate: some council-retained, some housing association, a growing share sold off under Right to Buy and now privately rented or owner-occupied. Fabric retrofit is supposed to turn these homes into "hidden batteries" a heat pump can coast through an evening demand peak without the tenant noticing. Does the physics support that, and if so, how much of it can a landlord actually deliver given who really controls the building?

## Finding 1: the retrofit's margin is real; the baseline's margin was a modelling artifact — and holds even once the model accounts for people actually living there

A single-node (1R1C) thermal model, treating the dwelling as an empty box with no internal heat gains, says a pre-1919 terrace on original fabric can coast for 4.7 hours before dropping from 22°C to a 19°C comfort floor at −3°C outdoor — enough to just clear the 4-hour evening peak window this project models (16:00–20:00). That was this project's original headline. Two later checks each found a real problem with it — one structural, one about what the model was and wasn't modelling.

The structural problem: a single-node model has no way to show indoor air cooling faster than the whole-building average implies, because it has no separate, small-capacity "air" node to do so — and air temperature, not a lumped average, is what a thermostat and an occupant actually respond to. The modelling gap: no allowance for the ~400W of constant heat that occupants, appliances, cooking and lighting actually generate (a bottom-up estimate cross-checked against ASHRAE occupant metabolic figures, PROVISIONAL).

Fixing both together gives the range this project now stands behind. A physically-grounded two-node (air/mass) model — using standard internal surface resistances from BS EN ISO 6946, the same standard used to build the U-values in the first place — with the 400W gain netted off every state's heating demand identically, then swept across the one genuinely ungrounded parameter (how much of the building's thermal capacity sits in the fast-responding air node, swept generously from 2% to 20%): baseline fabric's coastdown is **1.6–3.9 hours**. It does not reliably clear the peak window, and never does even at the top of the range. Wall-insulation-only fabric is **2.0–4.4 hours**, clearing the window only at the single most generous point in the sweep, not reliably. EPC-C fabric is **11.4–14.3 hours** — comfortably above the 4-hour window at every point in the same sweep. The retrofit's margin holds up under scrutiny; the baseline's apparent margin did not.

![Coastdown hours by envelope state, 1R1C headline vs 2R2C sensitivity range](./figures/coastdown_by_envelope_state.png)

The internal-gains mechanism is worth isolating on its own, purely as an illustration in the simpler single-node model — these single-node numbers are not the standing result, the two-node range above is. Before the structural correction, 400W stretches baseline's coastdown only modestly, from 4.7h to **5.0h**, since it's a small fraction of a large heat loss; against EPC-C's small heat loss, the same 400W is a much larger fraction and coastdown extends substantially, from 15.0h to **18.7h**. That asymmetry — gains matter more as a house gets tighter — is the mechanism the two-node range above already reflects; the single-node pair here just makes it visible in isolation. (400W is a flat average: real occupancy and cooking gains concentrate inside the evening peak specifically, which only strengthens this mechanism, while December solar gains through the windows are negligible and remain unmodelled.)

One number is unaffected by any of this, including the internal-gains revision, and structurally always will be: the steady-state electrical demand reduction, from 2.46 kW/home to 0.66 kW/home at an assumed heat pump COP of 2.5 — a **1.80 kW/home cut**, or **1.8 MW of avoided peak demand per 1,000 homes** retrofitted (2.62 kW and 0.82 kW before the gains correction — the absolute figures move, the 1.80 kW gap between them doesn't). Internal gains are applied identically to every fabric state, so they cancel out of any DIFFERENCE between two states even though they change each state's own absolute figures — this reduction depends only on heat-loss coefficient and heat pump efficiency, not on how thermal capacity is split between air and structure, nor on how much of a household's own heat the model accounts for.

**The numbers this project now stands behind:** baseline coastdown 1.6–3.9h (never clears the 4h window); wall-insulation-only 2.0–4.4h (clears only at the single most generous assumption); EPC-C 11.4–14.3h (clears throughout); electrical demand cut 2.46 kW → 0.66 kW at COP 2.5.

A related, separate review question was answered without needing a fix: was the baseline heat-loss coefficient (273 W/K) artificially low for a "truly leaky" unretrofitted pre-1919 terrace? No — 273 W/K already sits inside the 250-300 W/K range independently proposed as the expected figure, verified by direct hand calculation (215 W/K fabric + 58 W/K ventilation), not adjusted.

## Finding 2: today, most of the estate doesn't have this battery yet

Weighting across the actual tenure mix on a pre-1919 terrace estate — 24% local authority-retained, 41% housing association-retained, 15% ex-Right to Buy privately rented, 20% ex-Right to Buy owner-occupied — and each segment's real insulation probability (English Housing Survey 2024–25), **27% of the estate is solid-wall insulated today.** The other 73% is still on baseline fabric.

That 27% describes fabric capability, not necessarily delivered flexibility. The coastdown physics throughout this project assumes a "charged battery": before any curtailment begins, the home is modelled as already sitting at the 22°C VPP preheat ceiling Finding 1's coastdown runs from (`comfort_band.preheat_ceiling_c`, a deliberate 1°C pre-heat above the everyday 21°C living-room setpoint, `comfort_band.normal_setpoint_c`). Reaching that preheat ceiling at all presupposes the home was already being kept at something like its normal 21°C setpoint beforehand — that everyday setpoint, not the 22°C preheat figure, is what this behavioural caveat is actually about. Fuel poverty and prepayment-meter self-rationing concentrate in exactly the lowest-insulation tenure segments modelled here, so real occupant behaviour is plausibly anti-correlated with fabric quality: the homes least likely to be insulated are also plausibly least likely to be heated to a normal 21°C setpoint in the first place, let alone preheated a further degree for a VPP event. If so, usable capacity across the estate is lower than 27% suggests. This isn't modelled — no occupant behaviour data was used — but it sharpens the equity finding even as it weakens the flexibility-resource claim.

Propagating uncertainty in the genuinely uncertain physical inputs (heat pump COP, thermal capacity, dwelling geometry) through 5,000 Monte Carlo draws gives a peak-reduction range of **1.53–2.30 kW/home avoided (P10–P90), median 1.87 kW** — unchanged by the internal-gains revision above, for the same cancellation reason (Finding 1). The EPC-C coastdown-hours range from the same draws — single-node model with gains, the same base model the Monte Carlo itself is built on, not Finding 1's two-node structural sensitivity — is NOT unaffected, since it's an absolute figure rather than a difference between states: **13.1–23.5 hours (P10–P90), median 17.9h**, up from a pre-gains range of roughly 10.8–18.6h. (This is why the range sits above Finding 1's standing two-node EPC-C range of 11.4–14.3h — the two figures answer slightly different questions, model-structure and parameter-uncertainty respectively, not a discrepancy in either.) Neither range includes survey uncertainty in the tenure or insulation-rate figures themselves, which are treated as exact once cited.

## Finding 3: a specific street can look very different from the estate average

The 27% figure is an estate-wide expectation, not a promise about any one street. (A real LV feeder typically serves 100–300 homes; a 30-home group is better called a street or sub-feeder sample, and that's the language used throughout this project now.) Simulating 5,000 random 30-home streets drawn from the same tenure/insulation mix, the middle 80% range from 17% to 37% insulated — and the worst simulated street had 0%.

This simulation draws each street as an independent random sample. Real tenure is spatially autocorrelated — whole streets were sold off under Right to Buy together, rather than individual homes being randomly scattered across the estate — so real-world street-to-street variation is likely wider than this range, and a 0%-insulated street is likely more common in reality than this model suggests. Treat 17–37% as a lower bound on real-world clustering, not an upper one.

## Finding 4: the battery gets smallest when the weather is coldest, and again on specific streets — whether those two things coincide is an open question

Running the December 2022 UK cold snap progressively colder finds the point where baseline fabric starts failing to hold the comfort floor during the evening peak: **8°C colder** than the event actually was (outdoor minimum in the peak window at that point: −9.4°C). That's a wide gap from what actually happened — the recorded peak window (16:00–20:00) only ranged from about −1.4°C to +2.0°C across the whole week, well above the −3°C design case Finding 1's coastdown-hours are built around, which is exactly why zero breaches occur at the temperatures that actually occurred that week: baseline's thin margin was never tested against anything close to design-case cold in this particular event. This stress test runs the same single-node (1R1C) hour-by-hour simulation as the earlier cold-snap check (Finding 1's structural correction is a separate, static sensitivity that hasn't been wired into this dynamic simulation). That makes this stress test conservative in a specific sense: the single-node model coasts longer than the two-node one, so under Finding 1's standing coastdown ranges some original-fabric homes would likely breach even in the recorded week's milder window — the zero-breaches result understates today's exposure rather than overstating it. The simulation now correctly nets off the 400W internal gain throughout — an omission found and fixed during this review pass, which is what moved the breach point from a pre-fix −6°C to the current −8°C. At that breach point, a diversified street has 22 of 30 homes forced to resume heating simultaneously (~64.6 kW coincident spike); a street concentrated in ex-Right-to-Buy privately rented stock — the segment with the lowest insulation rate, at 10% — has 27 of 30 (~79.2 kW).

![Street exposure under the anti-correlation stress test](./figures/street_exposure_stress_test.png)

This shows the flexibility a VPP or DNO might count on shrinking exactly when the weather is coldest, and shrinking further on the streets where tenure fragmentation has concentrated the least-insulated stock. It does **not** show that those streets spatially coincide with a DNO's actual constrained feeders — network constraints driven by EV uptake, electrified heat, and weak LV assets could concentrate anywhere, independent of tenure history. That overlap is a plausible, testable hypothesis this project has not verified against real network data, not a finding it has established.

It's also worth being explicit that a coincident reheating spike isn't the end of the story: every hour of curtailment that ends in resumed heating displaces that demand to the recovery period rather than deleting it, and if cold snaps recur on consecutive days, the same homes may not fully recover comfort between events — a comfort-and-equity concern for exactly the vulnerable households this project is most concerned about, not just a grid one. Not quantified here.

## Finding 5: two honest numbers that tell different stories, and one that shouldn't be asked to answer a question it can't

Retrofitting this archetype to EPC-C costs **£10,728/home** (English Housing Survey, pre-1919 age-band average — not the cheaper £6,335 figure you get by blending in newer, easier-to-treat cavity-wall stock nationally, which understates the cost for this specific archetype).

Flexibility-service tenders (Piclo Flex and equivalent DNO/DSO procurement) require metered, dispatchable, verifiable demand reduction against a settled baseline. Passive fabric retrofit, on its own, is none of these things — it has no meter, no controllable asset, no dispatch signal, and cannot bid into these markets as modelled here. So the **£127/home/year** figure this project can compute from an illustrative flexibility rate (£68/kW/year) is not a realistic revenue estimate a landlord could collect; it's a "what would this be worth if it were contractible" illustration. Even taken at face value, it would need roughly **84 years** to repay the retrofit — it was never going to be the financial case, and now it's clearer why: the market mechanism it's priced against structurally cannot purchase what fabric alone delivers. The two channels that plausibly could — avoided DNO reinforcement capex, and direct energy bill savings — are not quantified in this project at all.

The more honest capacity comparison leads with what the model actually delivered, not a theoretical ceiling: during the real December 2022 event, the retrofit's usable thermal storage delivered about **2.2 kWh of curtailed demand per home** (down from an earlier 2.9 kWh once the model was corrected to account for the household's own internal heat gains — see Finding 1), worth roughly **£1,447/home** in battery-hardware-equivalent terms at current UK installed prices. The building's nominal thermal storage capacity — unaffected by internal gains, since it's a comfort-band calculation, not a heat-loss one — is unchanged at about 5x higher (12 kWh-equivalent, ~£7,800/home), and the real event now calls on under a fifth of it rather than a quarter. Leading with the bigger number would still have overstated what this specific retrofit, against this specific event, actually did — if anything, more so now.

## Finding 6: a battery+solar+VPP fit-out is a real, complementary asset — not a substitute, and not (yet) how this estate is actually adopting flexibility

The natural next question, once fabric's flexibility value turns out to be structurally non-contractible (Finding 5): how does that compare to an asset that genuinely *can* be metered, dispatched, and paid for today? A domestic battery's power rating (3.6–11.5 kW across real UK products) is never the constraint on covering this project's peak window, at any fabric state — even an unretrofitted home's 2.46 kW peak demand (net of internal gains, Finding 1) is comfortably within reach. The real constraint is energy: a standard battery — modelled here at its full 10 kWh nameplate figure as usable capacity, with no additional depth-of-discharge or round-trip-efficiency derating layered on top (this project's own OMITTED ledger, `PROJECT.md` §8.6, already flags battery degradation and round-trip losses as unmodelled; a real installer's usable-capacity figure, after DoD, would be lower than 10 kWh, which would only push these percentages higher) — has to supply 9.84 kWh, **98% of that 10 kWh**, every evening to fully cover an unretrofitted home's peak demand for the full 4-hour window, versus just 2.64 kWh, **26%**, for an EPC-C home — the same underlying physical mechanism as Finding 1, seen from the battery's side rather than the wall's.

![Battery energy needed for full peak-window coverage, by fabric state](./figures/battery_utilisation_by_envelope_state.png)

Unlike fabric's illustrative-if-contractible flexibility value, a BESS+solar fit-out's VPP-dispatch-plus-arbitrage revenue is real and currently contractible — roughly **£1,075/home/year**, against a bundled installed cost of about **£13,500/home**, a genuine **12.6-year** simple payback. That is a materially more honest economic case than fabric's, because the underlying market mechanism actually exists for it. Solar's own contribution to this specific winter-evening question is close to zero, worth stating plainly: the 16:00–20:00 peak window falls after dark in a December cold snap, so solar's economic value here is an annual, mostly-daytime/summer figure, not a peak-shaving one.

And yet, on this same estate, fabric retrofit adoption (27%) is roughly **4.6x higher** than BESS/solar adoption (an estimated 5.8%, weighted across tenure). That's a real inversion worth sitting with: the asset with no working revenue mechanism today has been deployed far more than the asset that has one — because fabric retrofit has been landlord-funded and programme-driven (the Warm Homes Social Housing Fund, ring-fenced council capital), while solar-plus-battery has historically been an individual owner-occupier purchase, not a landlord rollout. BESS/VPP turns out to have its own split-incentive problem, and a sharper one than fabric's: the ongoing VPP/arbitrage revenue typically accrues to whoever holds the electricity account — usually the tenant in a standard let — not the landlord who would have to fund the install, with no equivalent of fabric's EPC-compliance or asset-value benefit to compensate. Since February 2026, the Warm Homes Social Housing Fund allows landlords to add battery storage under the same grant mechanism as solar — too recent to show up in adoption data yet, and genuinely unknown whether it narrows this gap.

Put in landlord-finance terms rather than energy terms, this stops being a close call. Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards are being extended to the social rented sector for the first time: social landlords must reach one reformed EPC metric by 1 April 2030, a second by 2039, subject to a time-limited £10,000-per-property spend cap on the first deadline. The reformed Decent Homes Standard, confirmed January 2026, folds that same requirement into its own Criterion D (Thermal Comfort), with compliance required by 2035. A home that misses either of these is a live regulatory and financial exposure for the *landlord* specifically, not just discomfort for the tenant — and housing association stock is carried on the balance sheet at Existing Use Value-Social Housing (EUV-SH), a rental-income-based valuation that lenders assess loan covenants against. Fabric retrofit is what a landlord spends against that exposure: it moves the EPC band, satisfies Criterion D, and — reasoned here from that compliance exposure, not independently verified against a primary valuation methodology, so treat this specific link as inference rather than a sourced fact — plausibly protects the income stream EUV-SH is built on. A battery does none of this: it isn't a scored input in the EPC methodology this project's band framing relies on elsewhere, does nothing for the damp and mould newly assessed under the standard's own Criterion E, and protects no balance-sheet figure. Its financial upside is still real (Finding 6's own £1,075/year), but it still accrues to whoever holds the electricity account — usually the tenant, not the landlord funding the install. Fabric is, in other words, less an "energy investment" for an HA landlord than an asset-protection-and-compliance one, with the tenant's lower bills as a co-benefit; a battery is closer to a tenant appliance the landlord would be funding on the tenant's behalf. That difference — not the relative size of either asset's energy-market revenue — is probably the sharper explanation for a 4.6x adoption gap running in the direction it does.

The honest reading is not "BESS beats fabric" or vice versa. They answer different questions: fabric buys comfort, health, and EPC compliance with no real revenue mechanism; BESS/VPP buys real revenue and full peak coverage with no comfort or fabric benefit. Collapsing them into one blended ROI number would repeat the exact category error Finding 5 already corrected once.

## Finding 7: the cold snap proves the headroom exists; a whole year shows what it's actually worth

Findings 1–6 all sit inside a single −3°C design-condition event — useful for a DNO sizing local network headroom, but silent on the question a landlord, tenant, or funder actually asks: what does this retrofit displace, save, and abate over a full year? Running the same physics — the project's own `net_heating_power_kw` function, unchanged — across 12 monthly mean outdoor temperatures (Manchester, the same region as the cold-snap event), held at the everyday **21°C** living-room setpoint (`comfort_band.normal_setpoint_c` — not the 22°C VPP preheat ceiling Finding 1's coastdown physics runs from, which only applies during an active curtailment event, not as a year-round baseline), gives annual space-heating electricity of **9,671 kWh/yr** for baseline fabric, **7,966 kWh/yr** for wall-insulation-only, and **2,071 kWh/yr** for EPC-C — the same three states, same COP, same 400W internal gains netted off every month exactly as they are netted off every hour elsewhere in this project. This deliberately avoids importing an external heating-degree-day figure at a fixed base temperature, which would silently reintroduce the internal-gains inconsistency Finding 1 already corrected once: a fixed base temperature assumes uniform gains regardless of a state's own heat-loss coefficient, and this project's own headline result is that gains matter far more, proportionally, to a tight EPC-C home than a leaky baseline one.

![Annual heating electricity by fabric state](./figures/annual_impact_by_envelope_state.png)

The full retrofit (baseline→EPC-C) displaces **7,600 kWh/yr per home** — worth about **£1,984/yr** at the current Ofgem price cap (26.11 p/kWh), and about **1.0 tCO2e/yr** on the official average grid-carbon basis (DESNZ 2026, 0.131 kg CO2e/kWh). That carbon figure is genuinely basis-dependent, and this project deliberately does not pick a single headline: on a marginal-emissions basis — using CCGT-typical generation as a proxy, since no official long-run marginal emissions factor series exists for this purpose — the same displaced energy abates **2.7–3.0 tCO2e/yr**, roughly three times the average-grid figure. Which basis is "correct" for a retrofit-abatement claim is a genuinely contested question in the wider decarbonisation literature; reporting both, clearly labelled, is more honest than resolving a debate this project has no standing to settle.

Scaled the same way Finding 1's peak-demand figure was scaled to estate level: full retrofit of 1,000 homes would displace **7,600 MWh/yr**, save **≈£1.98m/yr**, and abate **≈996 tCO2e/yr** (average basis). Today's actual 27% uptake (Finding 2) delivers roughly a quarter of that — **≈2,054 MWh/yr, ≈£536,000/yr, ≈269 tCO2e/yr** — the same potential-versus-delivered gap Finding 2 already identified for peak demand, restated in the units a bill-payer or a carbon budget actually cares about.

Two summer-season risks sit outside everything above and are not modelled, only flagged, per this project's own Sledgehammer Test discipline (`PROJECT.md` §2.2) — building either properly would need weather, roof, and network data this project doesn't have. First, solar export and voltage rise: a retrofit programme that also adds rooftop solar (Finding 6) pushes power back onto the LV network at exactly the times statutory voltage limits (216–253V, ENA/NGED G98-G99) are most likely to bind — a real mechanism, not quantified against this archetype's own feeder. Second, summer overheating: retrofit that adds external wall insulation — the assumption used throughout this project (§8.3) — plausibly performs better on summer comfort than internal wall insulation would, per CIBSE TM59 and recent peer-reviewed literature, but that is a literature-sourced direction, not a modelled result for this specific dwelling. Neither risk changes the winter case above; both are real enough that a full annual or seasonal business case should name them rather than stay silent.

**The numbers this project now stands behind:** annual heating electricity 9,671 / 7,966 / 2,071 kWh/yr (baseline / SWI-only / EPC-C); full-retrofit annual displacement 7,600 kWh/yr, ≈£1,984/yr, ≈1.0 tCO2e/yr average or 2.7–3.0 tCO2e/yr marginal-proxy, per home; per-1,000-homes potential ≈7,600 MWh/£1.98m/996 tCO2e vs today's-27%-uptake ≈2,054 MWh/£536k/269 tCO2e (all average basis); summer solar-export/voltage risk and summer overheating risk both real, both literature-grounded, neither quantified here.

## Finding 8: over its working life, the retrofit doesn't just abate carbon cheaply — it pays for itself and keeps paying

Findings 5 and 7 are both annual or one-off snapshots: a capital cost (Finding 5) and an annual bill/carbon saving (Finding 7). Neither answers the question a cost-effectiveness comparison actually needs: what does this retrofit cost, or save, over the multi-decade life of the fabric itself? That requires a sourced lifetime, not a round-number guess — this project uses **36 years**, the Ofgem ECO/Green Deal "Appropriate Guarantees" standard measure life for solid wall insulation (cavity wall insulation is conventionally 42 years, mobile-home insulation 30; an earlier internal draft used an unsourced 40 before this was corrected).

The simple payback, from bill savings alone, is **5.4 years** (£10,728 ÷ £1,984/yr). That leaves roughly 30 more years of the fabric's working life in which the household keeps saving money with no further spend — £71,434 in lifetime bill savings against the original £10,728 outlay, a net **£60,706 saved**, not spent, over the measure's life.

That changes what "cost per tonne abated" should mean here. A gross figure — capital cost divided by lifetime carbon abated, ignoring the fact that the same spend also buys 30+ years of bill savings — comes to **£299/tCO2e** on the average-grid basis, or **£98–112/tCO2e** on the marginal-proxy basis. That's already a genuinely cheap figure by the standards of UK abatement measures generally. But it's not the right figure. A standard marginal abatement cost treatment nets the lifetime bill savings off the capital cost before dividing by carbon abated — fuel-cost savings are an offsetting benefit in any proper MACC, not something to set aside — and on that basis the cost is **negative**: **–£1,694/tCO2e** average, **–£555 to –£635/tCO2e** marginal. Negative means exactly what it says: this measure is cash-positive over its life even before any carbon value is counted at all. It sits in the "no-regrets" category of abatement, not merely the "cheap" one.

**The numbers this project now stands behind:** fabric life 36 years (GROUNDED, Ofgem ECO/Green Deal convention); simple payback 5.4 years; lifetime bill savings ≈£71,434 against a £10,728 outlay, net ≈£60,706 saved over the fabric's life; gross marginal abatement cost ≈£299/tCO2e average or £98–112/tCO2e marginal; net (bill-savings-adjusted) marginal abatement cost ≈–£1,694/tCO2e average or –£555 to –£635/tCO2e marginal — negative-cost, "no-regrets" abatement, not a sign error.

## What this project does not claim

This archetype — a mid-terrace — has the fewest exposed surfaces per home of any solid-wall form, which makes its physics a *favourable* case, not a representative one: semis, detached houses, and bungalows will coast faster and show a smaller margin than reported here. On the tenure axis this archetype is a hard case (fragmented ownership, expensive retrofit); on the physics axis it's an easy one. Results should not be assumed to transfer directly to other archetypes in either direction.

This project does not claim any specific real estate has been measured — it's a physics-based digital twin calibrated against national statistics, not a surveyed building. It does not model domestic hot water or occupant behaviour. Since Finding 7, it does cover whole-year space-heating electricity, bill savings, and carbon abatement — but the annual-energy method itself has a known conservative bias (monthly-mean temperatures understate true demand, more so for well-insulated states, versus a full degree-day or hourly method), and neither summer risk it flags (solar export/voltage rise, overheating) is quantified against this specific archetype. Its economics are illustrative, and — per Finding 5 — actively mis-priced if read as a market a landlord could sell into today. Its thermal model, even in its more careful two-node form, is still a simplification of a real building. Its internal-gains figure (Finding 1) is a flat constant, not a diurnal occupancy/cooking schedule — likely an understatement during the evening peak specifically, when cooking and occupancy typically run highest, and an overstatement overnight.

## Bottom line

The hidden battery is real, but a good part of what made it look bigger than it is turned out to be an artifact of the simplest version of the model. Under closer scrutiny, only the fully retrofitted state has a coastdown margin that survives; original fabric's apparent safety margin mostly does not. Three-quarters of the estate is still on that fragile fabric, the quarter that's missing is concentrated on exactly the streets a landlord has the least power to fix, and the market mechanism most people would reach for to pay for any of this cannot actually buy what passive fabric delivers. None of that changes the direction of the core finding — retrofit creates real, physically grounded grid value — but it changes how confidently any single number from this project should be repeated without its caveat attached.

Insulation was never going to replace the need for more generation or network capacity — it buys a real but bounded amount of demand-shifting headroom, not a substitute grid asset. Extending the model to a BESS+solar+VPP fit-out (Finding 6) sharpens that: a battery is a genuinely dispatchable, revenue-generating asset that fabric alone can never be, but on this same estate it has been adopted roughly 4.6x less than fabric — because the asset with a working revenue mechanism has so far been an individual consumer purchase, while the asset without one has been a landlord-funded programme. Both gaps — fabric's uneven, tenure-gated delivery, and BESS/solar's own sharper split-incentive problem — point at the same underlying issue this project keeps finding from different angles: the physics and the economics of flexibility are rarely the constraint. Who controls the capital, and who captures the benefit, usually is.

Widening from a single cold night to a full year (Finding 7) doesn't change any of that — it just shows the winter design-condition case was never the whole case. The same retrofit that buys a DNO headroom on the coldest evening of the year buys a household roughly £2,000 a year and abates somewhere between 1 and 3 tonnes of CO2e a year, honestly reported as depending on which grid-carbon basis you'd defend. Today's 27% uptake captures about a quarter of that potential — the same fragmented-delivery story as Finding 2, now priced in pounds and tonnes rather than kilowatts. Priced over the fabric's own working life rather than one year at a time (Finding 8), it's a stronger claim still: the retrofit is not simply cheap decarbonisation, it is decarbonisation that pays for itself within six years and then keeps paying for another three decades — the kind of result a marginal abatement cost curve would file under "no-regrets," not merely "affordable."
