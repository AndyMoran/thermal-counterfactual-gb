# Practical Implications: What This Means for You

`FINDINGS.md` reports what the model shows. This document translates those same, already-grounded numbers into "what's in it for me" for three audiences who would actually make a retrofit decision: CEOs and portfolio leaders, facilities and asset managers, and Housing Association leadership specifically. Every figure below is cited back to its Finding — nothing new is introduced here, only reframed. One genuinely promising extension — combined BESS+heat pump CapEx reduction from fabric-first sequencing — is flagged at the end as a real idea worth modelling properly, not yet a number this project can stand behind.

---

## For CEOs and portfolio leaders: this is a cash-positive balance sheet play, not an ESG cost

**Full fabric retrofit pays for itself in 5.4 years, then nets £60,706 per home, not spent, over the remaining 30 years of its working life** — against a £10,728 outlay, and using nothing but bill savings that already happen today, at today's electricity price (Finding 8). Scaled to a 1,000-home portfolio at full uptake, that's £1.98m/year in avoided bills; at today's actual 27% uptake, it's already running at roughly £536,000/year (Finding 7).

The actual volume, for anyone who has to report it: full retrofit abates **~1.0 tCO2e/home/year on the official average-grid basis (DESNZ 2026), or 2.7–3.0 tCO2e/home/year on a marginal-emissions basis** — roughly three times higher, because it reflects the gas-plant generation actually displaced at the margin rather than the whole grid's average mix (Finding 7). Scaled to a 1,000-home portfolio at full uptake: **~996 tCO2e/year average basis, or roughly 2,700–3,000 tCO2e/year marginal basis** (the marginal portfolio figure is this project's own direct scale-up of the per-home marginal number, not a separately stated result — flagged as such). At today's actual 27% uptake, that's already running at **~269 tCO2e/year average basis** (Finding 7). Which basis is "correct" is a genuinely unsettled question in the wider literature — this project deliberately reports both rather than picking one, and any board or SECR reporting built on this should do the same rather than quote whichever figure is more flattering.

The abatement case is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly: once lifetime bill savings are netted against the capital cost — the standard treatment in any real marginal abatement cost curve, not a favourable trick — the cost per tonne of carbon abated is **negative**: roughly –£1,694/tCO2e on an average-grid basis, or –£555 to –£635/tCO2e on a marginal-emissions basis (Finding 8). That means this measure is cash-positive over its life before any carbon value is counted at all. It belongs in the "no-regrets" category of capital allocation, not the "cheap decarbonisation" one — a materially easier case to put to a board or a lender than a typical ESG capex line.

There is also a real, dated compliance exposure sitting underneath this, not just an upside case. Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards are being extended to the social rented sector for the first time — one reformed EPC metric by 1 April 2030, a second by 2039 — and the reformed Decent Homes Standard folds the same requirement into its own Criterion D (Thermal Comfort), compliant by 2035 (Finding 6). A home that misses either deadline is a landlord-specific financial and regulatory exposure, not just a comfort issue for the tenant. Usefully for capital planning, the regulation itself puts a ceiling on this: a time-limited £10,000-per-property spend cap exempts landlords from being required to spend more than that to comply on the first deadline — a known ceiling on required capital, not an open-ended one.

*Caveat worth carrying into any board paper:* the link from EPC status to Existing Use Value-Social Housing (EUV-SH, the rental-income-based valuation lenders assess covenants against) is this project's own reasoned inference from real compliance exposure, not an independently sourced valuation-methodology finding. Directionally sound, not independently verified — say so if it's used.

## For facilities and asset managers: sequencing matters, and the retrofit does more than heat the building

The single most actionable operational fact in this whole project: **a post-retrofit (EPC-C) home needs only 26% of a standard 10kWh battery's capacity to cover the full evening peak window, versus 98% for an unretrofitted home** (Finding 6). That's not a marginal difference — it's the gap between a battery that's barely adequate and one with three-quarters of its capacity to spare. If fabric and battery rollouts are being planned as separate workstreams, doing fabric first changes what "adequate battery provision" even means for the homes that come after it.

The same physics shows up as avoided peak electrical demand: **2.46 kW/home drops to 0.66 kW/home at a heat pump COP of 2.5 — a 1.80 kW/home cut, or 1.8 MW of avoided peak demand per 1,000 homes retrofitted** (Finding 1). That's a real number for any conversation with a DNO about local network headroom, EV charging rollout capacity, or connection constraints on an estate.

Two spec-level considerations worth carrying into any retrofit programme, both real mechanisms but neither quantified for this specific archetype, so treat as things to check locally rather than settled facts: external wall insulation (EWI, the assumption used throughout this project) plausibly outperforms internal wall insulation on summer comfort, per CIBSE TM59 and recent literature; and adding rooftop solar at scale on one street pushes power back onto the LV network at exactly the times statutory voltage limits (216–253V, ENA/NGED G98-G99) are most likely to bind, which is worth a conversation with the DNO before phasing solar street-by-street rather than after (Finding 7).

Worth knowing before fielding "why haven't we done more battery/solar" questions: fabric retrofit already reaches 27% of this estate, while battery/solar sits at roughly 5.8% — not because fabric is more valuable, but because fabric has historically been landlord-funded and programme-driven, while battery/solar has been an individual owner-occupier purchase. The revenue a battery earns (roughly £1,075/home/year from VPP dispatch and arbitrage, a real 12.6-year payback) has typically accrued to whoever holds the electricity account — usually the tenant, not the landlord funding the install (Finding 6). Since February 2026, the Warm Homes Social Housing Fund allows landlords to fund battery storage under the same grant mechanism as solar — too recent to show up in adoption data, but the first real funding route that could close this gap.

## For Housing Association leaders: this is compliance and balance-sheet protection with a known price tag, not a discretionary energy spend

Two regulatory deadlines make this a "when," not an "if": one reformed EPC metric by 1 April 2030, a second by 2039 (MEES, extended to social housing for the first time), and Criterion D (Thermal Comfort) of the reformed Decent Homes Standard by 2035 (Finding 6). Housing association stock is carried on the balance sheet at Existing Use Value-Social Housing (EUV-SH) — a rental-income-based valuation lenders assess loan covenants against. A home that misses either deadline is a live exposure against that valuation, not just a discomfort issue for the tenant.

Fabric retrofit is what a landlord spends against that exposure: it moves the EPC band and satisfies Criterion D directly. A battery does neither — it isn't a scored input in the EPC methodology, does nothing for the damp and mould newly assessed under the standard's own Criterion E, and protects no balance-sheet figure, whatever its own real financial upside (Finding 6). For a compliance conversation specifically, fabric is the asset-protection spend; a battery is a separate, genuinely valuable but different investment.

On cost: the £10,728/home retrofit cost for this specific archetype (pre-1919 solid-wall, English Housing Survey age-band average — not the cheaper £6,335 blended national figure, which understates this archetype's real cost) pays back from bill savings alone in 5.4 years, and the £10,000-per-property MEES spend cap gives a known ceiling on what compliance can be required to cost on the first deadline (Finding 5, Finding 8). On funding: the Warm Homes Social Housing Fund (£1.29bn total programme) is a real, dated commitment already underpinning delivery, and its February 2026 extension to battery storage is the first mechanism letting landlords fund both fabric and battery under one grant route — worth raising directly in funding-strategy conversations, even though it's too recent to show whether it actually narrows the 27%-vs-5.8% adoption gap.

On carbon: since this Fund is itself decarbonisation-purposed, its own reporting will likely ask for tonnes, not just £/property. Full retrofit of a stock this size abates roughly **1.0 tCO2e/home/year (average-grid basis) or 2.7–3.0 tCO2e/home/year (marginal basis)** — see the CEO section above for the portfolio-scale figures and why both bases are reported rather than one being chosen (Finding 7).

One channel to actively rule out of any funding pitch: this project's illustrative flexibility-service revenue figure (£127/home/year, priced against a DNO tender rate) would take roughly 84 years to repay the retrofit on its own — it was never a realistic revenue case, because passive fabric has no meter and cannot bid into flexibility markets as modelled here (Finding 5). The real financial case for fabric is bill savings plus compliance-exposure avoidance, not flexibility-market income; don't build a board case on the wrong channel.

## Combined BESS + heat pump CapEx reduction — now checked, not just flagged

This was originally an unmodelled idea. It's now been checked directly (`src/check_combined_capex_hypothesis.py`), combining this project's own physics with real UK cost and grant data, and the result is genuine but more modest and more uncertain than the ~40% starting hypothesis.

Battery: solid. Finding 6's 98%→26% energy-need result maps onto real product tiers (10kWh-class baseline, 5kWh-class EPC-C) as roughly a **41% lower battery cost**, from multiple 2026 UK market surveys.

Heat pump: real, but weaker evidence and a smaller effect. This project's own physics gives a 73% reduction in required thermal output (6.15kW baseline → 1.65kW EPC-C) — but no domestic ASHP is sold at 1.65kW, so an EPC-C home still has to buy the smallest practical tier (4–6kW) rather than a unit sized to its true load. Triangulated from installer surveys (no official size-banded cost table exists), that's roughly **29% off the heat pump's cost** — real, but well short of what the thermal-load ratio alone implies, because of that practical size floor.

Combined: **~33% lower CapEx**, weighted by each component's own cost — in the same order of magnitude as the original 40% claim, but treat it as a range (roughly 25–40%) given how much less certain the heat pump half is.

Grant caps: this is where the check found something the original hypothesis didn't anticipate. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme gives a flat £7,500 toward an ASHP regardless of size — covering most of a right-sized unit's cost, a much smaller share of a full-size one. Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 has real per-property caps (£7,500 base, +£7,500 off-gas or up to £20,000-for-10%-of-homes on-gas), but its core scope is EPC bands D–G — a home already moved to EPC-C only qualifies for a restricted top-up route. Practical implication: bundle the heat pump into the *same* funding application as the fabric retrofit, not a later, separate one.
