# Addendum: Business-Case Translation and the Combined CapEx Check

**Status: post-freeze follow-up work, 14 August 2026.** `FINDINGS.md` and `PROJECT.md`'s core Assumptions Ledger represent this project's reviewed, settled record — the physics, the economics, and the caveats that went through multiple external review rounds. Nothing below changes any of it. This addendum exists separately, and deliberately, for two reasons: it answers a different question than `FINDINGS.md` does, and it isn't held to the same evidence standard throughout — which is exactly why it doesn't live in the main files.

## Why this exists

`FINDINGS.md` is technical and detailed by design — that rigor is what makes it trustworthy. It was never written to answer the question a CEO, facilities manager, or Housing Association leader actually asks: *so what does this mean for me, specifically?* `STAKEHOLDER_BRIEFS.md` and the LinkedIn series (`linkedin_article_business_case.md` and its three companion posts) are the answer to that — a translation layer linking the existing physics to practical financial and operational decisions, built entirely from figures already grounded in `FINDINGS.md`. Nothing new was modelled to produce them; they're a different lens on the same settled results.

## The combined CapEx check

This part is different in kind, not just in audience. A reader proposed a specific, sharper claim — that fabric-first sequencing cuts combined battery-plus-heat-pump CapEx by roughly 40%, helping Housing Association deals fit under grant caps. It was flagged as an unproven hypothesis in the first version of the business-case article, then actually checked (`src/check_combined_capex_hypothesis.py`) rather than left as a guess.

The result, at three separate confidence levels:

- **Heat pump right-sizing** — physics GROUNDED (this project's own `heat_loss_coefficient_w_per_k`, multiplied through by COP: baseline 6.15kW thermal vs EPC-C 1.65kW, a 73% reduction, consistent with `FINDINGS.md` Finding 1's published 2.46kW/0.66kW electrical figures). Costing PROVISIONAL, and the weakest-evidenced number in this whole addendum: no official UK size-banded ASHP cost table was found, only triangulated 2026 installer/aggregator surveys. Result: ~29% lower ASHP cost for EPC-C vs baseline — real, but well short of the 73% thermal-load reduction, because no domestic ASHP is sold near 1.65kW; the smallest practical tier (4–6kW) sets a floor EPC-C can't buy below.
- **Battery right-sizing** — PROVISIONAL costing, same evidentiary tier as `FINDINGS.md`'s existing battery-cost figures (multiple independent 2026 UK market surveys, discrete 5kWh/10kWh product tiers). Result: ~41% lower cost for EPC-C vs baseline — solid, and closely matches the original ~40% hypothesis on its own.
- **Combined** (weighted by each component's own cost, not averaged): ~33% lower CapEx. Same order of magnitude as the original claim, not a precise match — treat as a range of roughly 25–40% given the heat pump component's weaker costing.
- **Grant caps** — GROUNDED. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme gives a flat £7,500 toward an ASHP regardless of size (gov.uk/Ofgem), covering ~71% of baseline's estimated ASHP cost but ~100% of EPC-C's smaller unit's. Warm Homes: Social Housing Fund Wave 3 has real per-property cost caps — £7,500/home base (any measure, 50:50 match, any starting EPC band); +£7,500 for off-gas-grid low-carbon heating; up to £20,000/home for a 10%-of-application quota on-gas-grid (Menzies LLP, a specialist advisory firm working directly on WHSHF Wave 3 compliance, cross-checked against two independent secondary summaries). One finding the original hypothesis didn't anticipate: WHSHF Wave 3's core scope is EPC bands D–G — a home already moved to EPC-C only qualifies for further funding via the restricted 10%-quota/infill route. Practical implication: bundle heat pump and fabric into one funding application, not separate rounds.

Full detail and sourcing: `citations.md` (new entries appended at the end, in line with that file's own append-only, living-index design — unlike this addendum, it doesn't distinguish "frozen" from "new").

## What's still not done

A genuine combined-system-sizing optimisation (minimum-cost insulation+battery+heat pump combination for a given compliance/peak-coverage target) would be new modelling work beyond this check's two-point comparison. The ASHP costing specifically would benefit from a primary MCS/DESNZ size-banded cost table if one becomes accessible, rather than triangulated survey data — it's the one figure in this addendum that doesn't yet meet the rest of this project's own sourcing standard.

## The VPP-funded-battery ladder: Rung 2 (headroom-revenue scaling), 17 Aug 2026

A follow-on conversation asked a sharper question than the combined-CapEx check above: if fabric retrofit frees up spare battery capacity a VPP operator could dispatch for revenue, could that revenue justify a VPP funding the battery itself — for a Housing Association, at scale — rather than the landlord paying for it? Working through that idea surfaced a ladder of claims, each depending on the one below it, in the same spirit as this project's own history of not blending unchecked assumptions into settled findings:

1. **Right-size a battery for comfort alone** — already checked, above (~41% lower battery cost, 5kWh-class vs 10kWh-class).
2. **How does VPP/arbitrage revenue actually scale with spare capacity?** — checked here (`src/check_headroom_revenue_scaling.py`), because Finding 6's own £1,075/year VPP+arbitrage figure is applied identically across all three fabric states, and nothing before this check had actually tested whether that flat treatment is reasonable.
3. **Would a VPP profit from funding a battery in a Housing Association's stock in exchange for dispatch rights?** — re-tested here using Rung 2's result instead of the flat figure. Still a sketch, not a checked business case (see caveats below).
4. **Should a DNO co-fund it for a separate local-flexibility benefit?** — not yet checked; flagged in the conversation as needing its own revenue mechanism (a Piclo Flex-style local tender), not a share of arbitrage revenue, since national arbitrage dispatch timing and a DNO's local constraint timing aren't guaranteed to coincide.

**Rung 2's result:** the £1,075/year figure splits into two mechanically different revenue streams (Habo Energy market data, already cited): ~£210/year from short dispatch/frequency-response events (Axle Energy's ~£1/kWh rate), and ~£875/year from time-of-use arbitrage (overnight-cheap-charge, evening-peak-discharge). The arbitrage component competes for the *same* 16:00–20:00 window as comfort-holding, so it should scale with Finding 6's own spare-capacity percentages (2% / 18% / 74% by fabric state) rather than stay flat. The dispatch component is left unscaled — a flagged simplification, not a modelled result; real frequency-response events plausibly cluster during winter system-stress periods, which correlates with exactly when comfort-holding draw is highest (the same anti-correlation mechanism Finding 4 found for coastdown exposure), so treating it as fabric-state-independent is plausibly optimistic for leaky homes, not conservative.

Headroom-scaled revenue: **baseline ≈ £228/year, wall-insulation-only ≈ £368/year, EPC-C ≈ £858/year** — versus the flat £1,075/year used elsewhere in this project for all three states. The flat figure overstates baseline revenue by roughly 4.7x and wall-insulation-only by roughly 2.9x; for EPC-C it's close, understating by roughly 1.25x.

**Rung 3 re-tested:** a VPP funding a 10kWh battery (£5,500, this project's own existing battery-cost figure) in exchange for dispatch rights, using the headroom-scaled revenue instead of the flat estimate: **EPC-C payback ≈ 6.4 years** (vs a naive 5.1 years using the flat figure — still solidly economic, just more honestly stated), **wall-insulation-only ≈ 15.0 years** (marginal), **baseline ≈ 24.2 years** (clearly uneconomic). That baseline figure is the quantified version of the sequencing argument this project keeps arriving at from different angles — fabric has to come before a VPP has any commercial reason to fund battery capacity — now stated as a real number rather than an assertion.

**Battery-life figure sourced, 17 Aug 2026** (was: Andy's own unassessed 15-year assumption, flagged as unsourced above — same treatment as the earlier unsourced 40-year fabric-life guess later replaced with the cited 36-year Ofgem figure, Finding 8). Three tiers, not blended into one number:

- **GROUNDED, peer-reviewed**: Beltran, Ayuso & Pérez (2020), *"Lifetime Expectancy of Li-Ion Batteries used for Residential Solar Storage,"* Energies 13(3), 568, DOI 10.3390/en13030568 — semi-empirical ageing models run against real PV production and consumption profiles across multiple locations. Finds **7.44–18.37 years** to a 60%-remaining-capacity threshold. Wide range because it reflects genuine climate/usage variation, not imprecision.
- **PROVISIONAL, industry-standard, corroborating**: Aurora Solar — most LFP home batteries carry 10–15-year warranties guaranteeing 70–80% capacity retention, "generally expected to last 15 years or more" in practice. Cross-checked against Solar Insure's 6,000–10,000-cycle premium-LFP figure: at this project's own ~1.5 cycles/day arbitrage rate (Predbat documentation, cited above), that's ~11.0–18.3 years to a 70–80% threshold — a different capacity threshold than Beltran's, but a consistent order of magnitude.
- **Named, not used as a base case**: Kight PowerHub, a genuinely new (2026) Scottish-manufactured domestic battery, claims a 25-year warranty and 20,000-cycle life on a novel chemistry (Best Magazine, kightpowerhub.co.uk) — a real, specific claim, not marketing fluff, but a single vendor's own forward warranty commitment on a product with no long-run field track record yet, and well above every other source here. Using it as the anchor would repeat a mistake this project has already corrected elsewhere: accepting a vendor superlative at face value.
- **Excluded, category mismatch**: Kight's own off-grid *lighting* division separately cites a 20-year cell life (kightoffgrid.com) — a different product on a shallow single-nightly-discharge duty cycle, not comparable to a home battery doing ~1.5 deep arbitrage cycles a day. Same category-error discipline as not using the blended national £6,335 retrofit figure for this specific archetype (Finding 5).

Recomputing the EPC-C lifetime margin across the sourced range, not just the central estimate, changes the conclusion materially: at the **low (GROUNDED, pessimistic) end, 7.44 years, the margin after payback is only £880** — barely profitable, not the comfortable case the earlier central estimate implied. At the **central estimate, 15 years, £7,362**. At the **high (GROUNDED, optimistic) end, 18.37 years, £10,252**. The honest statement is that this business case is solidly profitable at the central-to-optimistic end of a real, sourced range, and only marginally profitable at the pessimistic end — not robustly profitable across the whole range, which is a materially more cautious conclusion than two turns ago. Also still not modelled: this treats revenue as flat until a cliff-edge end of life, when real degradation — and the arbitrage revenue that depends on remaining capacity — should decline gradually instead.

**The "silent default" scenario, 17 Aug 2026 addition:** a vertically-integrated installer-plus-VPP doesn't need to offer any revenue-sharing deal at all to capture the spare-capacity value — it can sell the battery purely on comfort/EPC-compliance/asset-protection grounds (all genuinely sufficient, per Finding 6), then privately enrol the same battery in its own aggregator arm and keep 100% of dispatch and arbitrage revenue, since nothing about the sale ever required disclosing or sharing it. Quantified in `src/check_headroom_revenue_scaling.py` §4: paying full capex (£5,500) and receiving none of the flexibility revenue (the uninformed default) is strictly worse than a VPP funding the battery outright (£0 capex, same £0 revenue to the landlord) — that gap alone is the full £5,500. Against the better alternative — the landlord pays the same capex but negotiates to keep the revenue itself — the gap in EPC-C stock at a central 15-year battery life is a further £12,862 in forgone lifetime revenue. Combined, the uninformed default costs up to **£18,362** relative to the best available structure, undiscounted, inheriting every caveat already flagged above (flat, not declining, revenue; a wide sourced battery-life range). Practical implication: who keeps flexibility-market revenue should be an explicit, negotiated term of any battery procurement, not an assumption — a pitch justified entirely on comfort and compliance grounds is exactly the situation where nobody is obliged to volunteer the answer.

**What Rung 2 doesn't settle:** the arbitrage-scales-with-headroom mechanism is PROVISIONAL, reasoned from Finding 6's already-grounded percentages plus installer-marketing-adjacent 2026 UK market guides (not a dispatch-log-verified result); the dispatch component's unscaled treatment is a named simplification with a plausible-but-unquantified downside risk in leaky homes; the "silent default" figures assume the installer/VPP has no other legitimate costs or risk absorbed by keeping the revenue (e.g. covering degradation/warranty risk itself), which hasn't been checked either way; and Rung 4 (DNO co-funding) remains unmodelled entirely. Carbon framing worth stating plainly, since it motivated this whole thread: none of this ladder changes the underlying carbon case for fabric retrofit, which stands on Findings 7–8 regardless of how the battery financing question resolves — this ladder is about who pays for, and who keeps the value from, the flexibility layer on top of an already-justified retrofit, not a precondition for it.
