A below-standard EPC is about to become a landlord-specific financial exposure, not just a tenant comfort issue.

Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards are being extended to social housing for the first time: one reformed EPC metric by 2030, a second by 2039. The reformed Decent Homes Standard folds the same requirement into Criterion D, compliant by 2035. Housing association stock sits on the balance sheet at Existing Use Value-Social Housing — a rental-income-based valuation lenders assess covenants against. (Worth being upfront: the specific link from EPC status to that valuation is this project's own reasoned inference from the compliance exposure, not an independently sourced valuation finding — directionally sound, treat it as that.)

Fabric retrofit is what moves the EPC band and satisfies Criterion D directly. A battery doesn't touch either.

On price: £10,728/home for this archetype, paid back from bills alone in 5.4 years — and a £10,000/property spend cap that's a known ceiling on what compliance can cost, not an open-ended one. On funding: the Warm Homes Social Housing Fund's February 2026 extension to battery storage is the first route letting landlords fund fabric and battery under one grant mechanism.

One channel worth ruling out of any funding pitch: the illustrative flexibility-service revenue this model can compute (£127/home/year) would take ~84 years to repay a retrofit. Passive fabric has no meter — it can't earn that way. The real case is bills plus compliance-exposure avoidance, not flexibility income.
